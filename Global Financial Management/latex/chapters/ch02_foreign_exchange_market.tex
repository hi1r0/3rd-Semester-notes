\section{Structure and Participants of the Foreign Exchange Market}
\lecturedate{07/09/2026}

\begin{definition}{Foreign Exchange Market}{def_forex_market}
The \textbf{Foreign Exchange (Forex) Market} is the global organizational and institutional framework where the currency of one country is exchanged for the currency of another country.
\end{definition}

\noindent In the foreign exchange market, currencies are quoted against a single base unit of another currency (e.g., $1\,\text{USD} = 95.88\,\text{INR}$). The market operates on a continuous, decentralized over-the-counter (OTC) basis across global financial centers.

\subsection{Core Market Participants}
The foreign exchange market operates through four constituent participant categories:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Commercial Banks and Central Banks:} Authorized dealer commercial banks serve as market makers by quoting two-way prices (bid and ask). Central banks participate to execute sovereign monetary policy, hold national reserves, and stabilize volatile domestic exchange rates.
    \item \textbf{Wholesale Foreign Exchange Brokers:} Intermediaries operating between commercial banks in the interbank tier, matching buy and sell orders rapidly while preserving market anonymity.
    \item \textbf{Exporters and Importers:} Commercial entities requiring foreign exchange conversion to settle merchandise and service trade transactions across international borders.
    \item \textbf{Multinational Corporations (Corporates):} Global enterprises transferring capital across jurisdictions for direct investment, cross-border dividend repatriation, and liquidity hedging.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Foreign Exchange Quotation Mechanics and Settlement Horizons}
\lecturedate{15/09/2026}

\begin{definition}{Foreign Exchange Quotation}{def_quotation}
A \textbf{Foreign Exchange Quotation} is the price of one currency expressed in units of another currency.
\end{definition}

\subsection{Delivery Horizons: Spot vs. Forward Rates}
Foreign exchange rates are quoted depending on the scheduled delivery date of the underlying currencies:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Spot Rate:} The exchange rate quoted for immediate delivery and settlement of currencies. In international banking practice, spot delivery is settled \textbf{two working days} after the date of the deal ($T+2$).
    \item \textbf{Forward Rate:} The exchange rate agreed upon today for the exchange of currencies at a specified future date (exceeding two working days from the transaction date).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Types of Exchange Rate Quotations}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Direct Quotation (Home Currency Quotation):} Expresses the price of \textbf{one unit of foreign currency} in terms of variable units of home currency.
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Home Currency (Variable)} = 1 \text{ Unit of Foreign Currency (Fixed)}
    \end{equation}
    \textit{Example in India:} $95.88\,\text{INR} = 1\,\text{USD}$ represents a direct quotation for the US Dollar in India.
    
    \item \textbf{Indirect Quotation (Foreign Currency Quotation):} Keeps the home currency fixed at \textbf{one unit} and expresses the exchange rate as variable units of foreign currency.
    \begin{equation}
        1 \text{ Unit of Home Currency (Fixed)} = \text{Foreign Currency (Variable)}
    \end{equation}
    \textit{Example:} $1\,\text{USD} = 0.87\,\text{EUR}$ represents an indirect quotation from the US domestic perspective.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Bid-Ask Spread and Percentage Spread}
Foreign exchange quotes consist of a two-way price quoted by market makers:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Bid Price:} The price at which the bank/market maker is willing to \textbf{buy} foreign currency.
    \item \textbf{Ask Price (Offer):} The price at which the bank/market maker is willing to \textbf{sell} foreign currency.
    \item \textbf{Spread:} The gross margin earned by the quoting dealer:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Spread} = \text{Ask Price} - \text{Bid Price}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Percentage Spread:} The proportional transaction cost relative to the asking price:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Percentage Spread (\%)} = \frac{\text{Ask Price} - \text{Bid Price}}{\text{Ask Price}} \times 100
    \end{equation}
\end{itemize}

\begin{example}{Bid-Ask Spread Calculation}{ex_bid_ask_spread}
\textbf{Problem Statement:}\\
In the interbank foreign exchange market, the US Dollar is quoted as:
\begin{equation*}
    1\,\text{USD} = \text{Rs. } 95.0000 - 95.0050
\end{equation*}
Calculate the absolute spread and the percentage spread for this quotation.

\tcblower
\textbf{Solution:}\\
From the quotation:
\begin{align*}
    \text{Bid Price} &= \text{Rs. } 95.0000 \\
    \text{Ask Price} &= \text{Rs. } 95.0050
\end{align*}

\noindent\textbf{1. Absolute Spread:}
\begin{equation*}
    \text{Spread} = \text{Ask} - \text{Bid} = 95.0050 - 95.0000 = \text{Rs. } 0.0050
\end{equation*}

\noindent\textbf{2. Percentage Spread:}
\begin{align*}
    \text{Percentage Spread} &= \frac{\text{Ask} - \text{Bid}}{\text{Ask}} \times 100 \\[0.3em]
    &= \frac{0.0050}{95.0050} \times 100 = 0.00526\%
\end{align*}
\textit{Note:} If the quotation were $95.0000 - 95.0140$, the absolute spread would be $0.0140$, yielding a percentage spread of approximately $0.014\%$.
\end{example}

\subsection{Cross Rates}
\begin{definition}{Cross Rate}{def_cross_rate}
A \textbf{Cross Rate} is the exchange rate between two non-home currencies, derived through their respective exchange rates against a common vehicle currency (typically the US Dollar).
\end{definition}
\noindent\textit{Example:} In the Indian market where INR is the domestic currency, quotes for $\text{EUR}/\text{USD}$, $\text{GBP}/\text{USD}$, or $\text{JPY}/\text{CNY}$ represent cross-rate transactions.

    \item \textbf{Forward Rate ($T > 2$):} Delivery of currencies occurs at an agreed future date beyond two working days.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{SWIFT (Society for Worldwide Interbank Financial Telecommunication)}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Organizational Nature:} A bank-owned cooperative society registered in \textbf{Brussels, Belgium}.
    \item \textbf{Ownership \& Scope:} Formed by 250 member banks in Europe and North America; links more than \textbf{25,000 financial institutions} worldwide.
    \item \textbf{Bank Identifier Codes (BIC):} Allots standardized identification codes (SWIFT/BIC codes) to each financial institution for routing.
    \item \textbf{Services:}
    \begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
        \item International payment instructions.
        \item Account statements from SWIFT.
        \item Specialized administrative and trade finance messages.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Indian Operations:} The primary regional processing center for India is situated in \textbf{Mumbai}.
    \item \textbf{Key Operational Advantages:}
    \begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
        \item Provides a reliable, secure method of transmitting messages across vast global locations.
        \item Guarantees high accuracy and cryptographic authentication.
        \item Enables counterparties to respond and process payments immediately.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{CHIPS (Clearing House Interbank Payments System)}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Organizational Nature:} A computerized electronic payment system owned by commercial banks constituting the \textbf{New York Clearing House Association}.
    \item \textbf{Historical Commencement:} Began operations in \textbf{1971}.
    \item \textbf{Operational Scope:} Represents the largest private-sector payment clearing system in the world. It clears foreign exchange and cross-border US Dollar transactions.
    \item \textbf{Settlement Mechanism:} Daily electronic netting and settlement of payments and receipts without physical checks (purely paperless interbank settlement).
\end{itemize}

\section{Market Terminologies, Margins, and Transaction Classifications}
\lecturedate{17/09/2026}

\subsection{Key Market Terminologies}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Market Maker:} Major commercial banks that stand ready to buy and sell currencies at publicly quoted prices, providing two-way market liquidity.
    \item \textbf{Two-Way Quotation:} A quotation indicating two distinct prices:
    \begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
        \item Price at which the market maker is willing to \textbf{buy} foreign currency (Bid).
        \item Price at which the market maker is willing to \textbf{sell} foreign currency (Ask).
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Forward Margin / SWAP Points:} The difference between the forward exchange rate and the spot exchange rate.
    \item \textbf{Forward Premium:} Occurs when foreign currency is costlier under the forward rate than under the spot rate ($\text{Forward Rate} > \text{Spot Rate}$).
    \item \textbf{Forward Discount:} Occurs when foreign currency is cheaper for forward delivery than for spot delivery ($\text{Forward Rate} < \text{Spot Rate}$).
    \item \textbf{Base Rate:} The ongoing interbank exchange rate on the basis of which commercial banks calculate merchant customer rates.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Golden Rules: The Banker's Perspective}
When analyzing any foreign exchange transaction, two fundamental principles must be strictly observed:
\begin{formulabox}[The Two Foundational Principles of Foreign Exchange Accounting]
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Banker's Point of View:} All transactions are designated strictly from the perspective of the \textbf{bank}, not the customer.
    \item \textbf{Underlying Asset:} The item being bought or sold is always the \textbf{Foreign Currency}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{formulabox}

\noindent Consequently, transactions are classified as:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Purchase Transaction:} The bank has \textbf{purchased foreign currency} (the bank receives foreign currency and pays home currency/INR to the customer).
    \item \textbf{Sale Transaction:} The bank has \textbf{sold foreign currency} (the bank parts with foreign currency and receives home currency/INR from the customer).
\end{itemize}

\lecturedate{18/09/2026}
\begin{example}{Classification of Foreign Exchange Transactions}{ex_purchase_sale}
\textbf{Problem Statement:}\\
Based on the banker's perspective, determine whether each of the following transactions constitutes a \textbf{Purchase} or a \textbf{Sale} of foreign exchange:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item The bank issues a Demand Draft (DD) on London for $100\,\text{EUR}$.
    \item A customer of the bank purchases a Telegraphic Transfer (TT) on New York for $500\,\text{EUR}$.
    \item A traveller encashes a traveller's cheque for $50\,\text{EUR}$ at the bank counter.
    \item The bank purchases a Demand Draft (DD) drawn on London for $500\,\text{EUR}$.
\end{enumerate}

\tcblower
\textbf{Solution:}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Sale of Foreign Exchange:} In issuing a DD on London, the bank sells foreign currency ($100\,\text{EUR}$) to the customer and receives rupees.
    \item \textbf{Sale of Foreign Exchange:} By executing a TT remittance to New York, the bank parts with foreign currency ($500\,\text{EUR}$) and receives rupees from the remitter.
    \item \textbf{Purchase of Foreign Exchange:} When encashing the traveller's cheque, the bank acquires/buys foreign currency ($50\,\text{EUR}$) and pays equivalent rupees to the traveller.
    \item \textbf{Purchase of Foreign Exchange:} By purchasing a draft drawn on London, the bank buys foreign currency ($500\,\text{EUR}$) from the presenter.
\end{enumerate}
\end{example}

\subsection{Calculation of Forward Rates under Direct Quotation}
In the interbank market, forward margins are quoted as points:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Ascending Order Margins $\implies$ Premium:} If forward margins are quoted in ascending order (e.g., $0.2000 / 0.2100$), foreign currency is at a \textbf{premium}. Margins are \textbf{added} to the spot rate:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Forward Rate} = \text{Spot Rate} + \text{Forward Premium}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Descending Order Margins $\implies$ Discount:} If forward margins are quoted in descending order (e.g., $0.6000 / 0.5700$), foreign currency is at a \textbf{discount}. Margins are \textbf{deducted} from the spot rate:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Forward Rate} = \text{Spot Rate} - \text{Forward Discount}
    \end{equation}
\end{itemize}

\begin{example}{Determining Forward Margins via Covered Interest Parity}{ex_cip_forward}
\textbf{Problem Statement:}\\
The spot rate for US Dollars in the Mumbai interbank market is $1\,\text{USD} = 95\,\text{INR}$. The annual rate of interest in India is $8\%$ p.a., while in New York it is $5\%$ p.a. A bank is asked to quote a three-month selling rate to a customer for $10,000\,\text{USD}$, assuming the entire interest differential gain or loss is passed on to the customer. Calculate the theoretical forward rate and forward premium.

\tcblower
\textbf{Solution:}\\
Given:
\begin{align*}
    S &= 95\,\text{INR/USD}, \quad r_{\text{INR}} = 8\% = 0.08, \quad r_{\text{USD}} = 5\% = 0.05, \quad t = \frac{3}{12} = 0.25\,\text{years}
\end{align*}

\noindent\textbf{1. Accounting Cash Flow Schedule (for USD 10,000):}
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{Xr}
\toprule
\textbf{Particulars} & \textbf{Amount} \\
\midrule
Purchase Dollar (Foreign Inflow) & 10,000.0000\,\text{USD} \\
Interest earned for 3 months @ 5\% p.a. ($10,000 \times 0.05 \times \frac{3}{12}$) & 125.0000\,\text{USD} \\
\midrule
\textbf{Total Foreign Currency Realization at Maturity} & \textbf{10,125.0000\,USD} \\[0.6em]
Borrow in Mumbai to pay for USD @ INR 95 ($95 \times 10,000$) & 950,000.0000\,\text{INR} \\
Interest payable for 3 months @ 8\% p.a. ($950,000 \times 0.08 \times \frac{3}{12}$) & 19,000.0000\,\text{INR} \\
\midrule
\textbf{Total Rupee Outflow to be Recovered (Rate Quoted)} & \textbf{969,000.0000\,INR} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\noindent\textbf{2. Theoretical Forward Rate (Exact Covered Interest Parity):}
\begin{equation*}
    F = \frac{\text{Total INR Outflow}}{\text{Total USD Realization}} = \frac{969,000.0000}{10,125.0000} = \textbf{95.7037\,INR/USD}
\end{equation*}
This matches the exact formula:
\begin{equation*}
    F = S \times \left[ \frac{1 + r_{\text{INR}} \times t}{1 + r_{\text{USD}} \times t} \right] = 95 \times \frac{1.0200}{1.0125} = 95.7037\,\text{INR}
\end{equation*}

\noindent\textbf{3. Linear Interest Differential Approximation:}
\begin{equation*}
    F_{\text{linear}} \approx S \times [1 + (r_{\text{INR}} - r_{\text{USD}}) \times t] = 95 \times [1 + (0.08 - 0.05) \times 0.25] = \textbf{95.7125\,INR}
\end{equation*}

\noindent\textbf{4. Forward Premium Difference:}
\begin{equation*}
    \Delta = 95.7125 - 95.7037 = \mathbf{0.0088\,INR}
\end{equation*}
The forward quotation reflects a forward premium of $0.0088\,\text{INR}$ per USD over the exact parity rate.
\end{example}

\subsection{Classification of Buying Rates}
Authorized dealers in India quote two standard types of buying rates:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Telegraphic Transfer (TT) Buying Rate:} Applied when the transaction does not involve any delay in the bank obtaining reimbursement in foreign currency.
    \item \textbf{Bill Buying Rate:} Applied when the bank purchases or discounts clean or documentary commercial trade bills.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{TT Buying Rate Mechanics}
The TT Buying Rate is applied in three distinct operational situations:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Payment of foreign Demand Drafts (DD), Mail Transfers (MT), and Telegraphic Transfers (TT) drawn on the quoting bank.
    \item Payment of proceeds of foreign export bills sent for collection, after realization overseas.
    \item Cancellation of foreign exchange forward sale contracts entered earlier with the customer.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent\textbf{Quotation Formula and FEDAI Rounding Rule:}
\begin{equation}
    \text{TT Buying Rate} = \text{Interbank Spot Buying Rate (Bid)} - \text{Exchange Margin}
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, noitemsep]
    \item The exchange margin is \textbf{deducted} because the bank pays fewer rupees to maximize its spread when buying foreign currency.
    \item \textbf{Rounding Convention:} In India, all merchant exchange rates must be rounded off to the \textbf{nearest multiple of 0.0025}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Bill Buying Rate Mechanics}
The \textbf{Bill Buying Rate} applies when a bank purchases or discounts a foreign trade bill. Because the bank disburses funds to the domestic exporter immediately while foreign reimbursement will only occur after the normal transit period (and usance period, if applicable), the forward rate for the transit period is used:

\begin{equation}
    \text{Bill Buying Rate} = \text{Forward Buying Rate (for Transit)} - \text{Exchange Margin}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Forward Margin Treatment under FEDAI Rules:}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Foreign Currency at a Premium (Ascending Margins):} The bank gives \textbf{no benefit} of forward premium to the customer and bases the quote on the Spot Rate.
    \item \textbf{Foreign Currency at a Discount (Descending Margins):} The forward discount for the full transit period (or transit maturity month) is \textbf{deducted} from the spot buying rate.
\end{itemize}

When commercial banks purchase or discount bills from customers, they advance rupee funds immediately. The bank claims interest from the customer covering the period from the purchase date until actual foreign realization:
\begin{equation}

\end{equation}
\noindent where $r$ is the annual bank interest rate, $\text{NTP}$ is the Normal Transit Period in days, and $\text{Usance}$ is the usance duration of the bill in days.

\section{Ready Rates Based on Cross Rates and Draft Cancellations}
\lecturedate{22/09/2026}

\subsection{Principle of Cross-Rate Quotations}
In the domestic Indian foreign exchange market, direct quotations are quoted for the US Dollar (USD/INR). However, exchange rates for other major currencies (such as the Canadian Dollar, Euro, Japanese Yen, or Swiss Franc) are derived by authorized dealer commercial banks based on rates prevailing in major international foreign exchange centers, including Singapore, London, Tokyo, and Hong Kong.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{USD-Base International Quotations:} In global financial markets, currencies are predominantly quoted in terms of the US Dollar as the base or terms currency (e.g., $1\,\text{USD} = 1.4551 / 1.4561\,\text{CAD}$, $1\,\text{USD} = 0.8541 / 0.8561\,\text{EUR}$, $1\,\text{USD} = 104.67 / 104.70\,\text{JPY}$).
    \item \textbf{Conversion to Indian Rupee Terms:} To quote a ready merchant rate to a domestic customer, the authorized dealer synthesizes the international USD quote with the domestic interbank USD/INR rate via cross-currency arithmetic.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Forward Margins and Swap Points}
In international markets, forward margins are quoted as points (swap points):
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Point Definition:} A point represents the last decimal position in the exchange quotation.
    \item \textbf{Four-Digit Quotations:} A point equals $0.0001$. For example, a 1-month forward margin quoted as $50/60$ on a spot rate of $1.5425/40$ represents $0.0050 / 0.0060$. Because margins are in ascending order ($50 < 60$), foreign currency is at a \textbf{forward premium} and margins are added to the spot base.
    \item \textbf{Two-Digit Quotations:} A point equals $0.01$. For example, a 1-month forward margin quoted as $17/16$ on a spot base of $104.67/70$ represents $0.17 / 0.16$. Because margins are in descending order ($17 > 16$), foreign currency is at a \textbf{forward discount} and margins are deducted from the spot base.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Mechanics of Foreign Demand Draft Cancellation}
When an authorized dealer commercial bank issues a foreign currency Demand Draft (DD) on an overseas center, the transaction constitutes a \textbf{Sale of Foreign Exchange}, quoted at the prevailing \textbf{TT Selling Rate}.

If the purchaser subsequently requests cancellation of the draft and repayment of the rupee equivalent, the bank must repurchase the foreign currency from the customer. Consequently:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Applicable Cancellation Rate:} All draft cancellations are executed strictly at the \textbf{TT Buying Rate}.
    \item \textbf{Cross-Rate TT Buying Formula:}
    To purchase foreign currency (FC) via cross rates, the bank buys USD locally at the Spot Bid rate (less exchange margin) and sells USD overseas at the market Ask (selling) rate to buy FC:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{FC TT Buying Rate} = \frac{\text{Interbank USD/INR Spot Bid} - \text{Exchange Margin}}{\text{Overseas Market USD/FC Ask Rate}}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Customer Gain or Loss on Cancellation:}
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Net Gain / Loss} = \text{Cancellation Proceeds (INR)} - \text{Original Purchase Cost (INR)}
    \end{equation}
    A negative result represents a loss to the customer (due to merchant spreads and margin loadings), whereas a positive result indicates an exchange gain from currency appreciation.
\end{itemize}

\section{Forward Exchange Contracts and FEDAI Rule 7}
\lecturedate{24/09/2026}

\subsection{Concept and Economic Purpose of Forward Contracts}
A \textbf{Forward Exchange Contract (FEC)} is an operational risk management instrument designed to provide complete protection to an importer or exporter against adverse fluctuations in foreign exchange rates (exchange rate risk).

\begin{definition}{Forward Exchange Contract (FEC)}{def_fec}
A \textbf{Forward Exchange Contract} is a legally binding agreement entered into between an authorized dealer bank and a customer (or between two commercial banks in the interbank tier) to buy or sell a specified amount of foreign currency at a predetermined exchange rate on an agreed future date or within an agreed future period.
\end{definition}

By booking an FEC, commercial counterparties lock in their conversion rates at inception:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Exporters (Forward Sale / Bank Purchases):} Eliminate the risk of foreign currency depreciation, fixing the exact rupee cash realization upon receipt of export proceeds.
    \item \textbf{Importers (Forward Purchase / Bank Sells):} Eliminate the risk of foreign currency appreciation, establishing the exact rupee expenditure required to settle overseas import payables.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Delivery Date Computation under FEDAI Rule 7}
A fundamental statutory standard established by FEDAI (Foreign Exchange Dealers' Association of India) governs the contractual maturity and delivery date of all forward exchange contracts booked in India:

\begin{center}
\begin{minipage}{0.92\linewidth}
\begin{remarkbox}[FEDAI Rule 7: Commencement of Forward Duration]
Under \textbf{FEDAI Rule 7}, the delivery date of a forward contract is deliverable at a future date where the contract duration is computed strictly from the \textbf{Spot Value Date ($T+2$)} of the transaction, and \textbf{NOT} from the trade booking date ($T$).
\end{remarkbox}
\end{minipage}
\end{center}

\noindent\textbf{Operational Example:}
If a customer books a 3-month forward exchange contract on \textbf{12th February}:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item The trade date is $T = \text{12th February}$.
    \item The spot value date is $T+2 = \text{14th February}$ (two business days post-trade).
    \item The 3-month contractual tenor commences on \textbf{14th February}, and the forward contract matures exactly on \textbf{14th May}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Classification: Fixed vs. Option Forward Contracts}
Depending on the flexibility of the settlement date, forward exchange contracts are categorized into two structural types:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Fixed Forward Contract:}
    A forward contract where the delivery and physical settlement of foreign exchange must take place strictly on a \textbf{specified future calendar date}.
    \begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
        \item \textit{Example:} On 5th March, a customer enters into a 3-month fixed forward sale contract for $10,000\,\text{EUR}$. Computing from the spot date (7th March), the contract matures on \textbf{7th June}. The customer must present the underlying bill or foreign exchange instrument to the bank exactly on 7th June. Delivery cannot occur prior to or later than this designated date.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Option Forward Contract:}
    A forward contract where the customer possesses the contractual option to deliver or take delivery of foreign exchange on \textbf{any business day during a specified contractual window} at the predetermined exchange rate.
    \begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
        \item \textit{Example:} On 15th September, a customer enters into a 2-month forward contract with "option over November". The customer is entitled to deliver foreign exchange to the bank on any working day between \textbf{1st November and 30th November}.
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{FEDAI Rule 7 Statutory Option Window Constraint:}
Under FEDAI Rule 7, the option delivery period granted to the customer shall \textbf{not exceed one calendar month}. Option contracts cannot span indefinite or multi-month settlement windows.

\subsection{Statutory Terms and Conditions of Forward Contracts}
All forward exchange contracts entered into by authorized dealers in India are governed by standard terms and conditions prescribed under FEDAI rules:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Commercial Backing:} Every forward exchange contract booked by a customer must be supported by genuine, underlying foreign trade exposure (firm export order, import letter of credit, or authorized external commercial borrowing).
    \item \textbf{Contractual Details:} The contract confirmation note must unambiguously state the contract date, counterparty name, currency, contract amount, agreed forward exchange rate, spot value baseline, and exact delivery horizon.
    \item \textbf{Customer Margin Requirements:} Banks may stipulate an initial cash margin or credit line to protect against potential default risk arising from adverse currency movements prior to settlement.
    \item \textbf{Non-Performance and Cancellation:} If the customer fails to deliver foreign exchange on the maturity date (or by the end of the option delivery window), the contract must be automatically cancelled by the bank on the 15th day post-maturity (or as prescribed by prevailing FEDAI rules) at the ongoing merchant buying/selling rate, with any resulting exchange loss recovered from the customer.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Forward Merchant Rates and Delivery Option Mechanics}
\lecturedate{08/10/2026}

\subsection{Mechanics of Forward Merchant Bill Buying Rates}
When an authorized dealer commercial bank enters into a forward purchase contract for an export bill, the date on which foreign exchange proceeds are physically realized depends on the classification of the underlying instrument:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Demand Bill:} Foreign currency funds are realized post-shipment after the \textbf{Normal Transit Period (NTP)}, standardly stipulated under FEDAI rules as \textbf{25 days}.
    \item \textbf{Usance Bill:} Realization occurs only upon maturity of the credit period, encompassing both the \textbf{Normal Transit Period} and the agreed \textbf{Usance Period} (e.g., $25 \text{ days transit} + 60 \text{ days usance} = 85 \text{ days}$).
\end{itemize}

Consequently, when quoting a forward merchant bill buying rate, the total duration for which the quoting bank must cover itself in the interbank market is:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Total Interbank Cover Tenor} = \text{Forward Period} + \text{Transit Period} + \text{Usance Period}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Statutory FEDAI Rules for Forward Swap Margins}
Because interbank forward margins are quoted on a monthly basis, fractional holding periods (in days) must be rounded to an integer number of months. To ensure the quoting bank remains fully hedged and does not grant undue speculative gain to the merchant:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Ascending Swap Margins (Foreign Currency at Forward Premium):}
    Adding a premium increases the buying rate paid to the exporter. Under FEDAI guidelines, the bank must give the minimum benefit to the customer by \textbf{rounding down} the total duration to the \textbf{lower whole month} (e.g., 25 days transit $\to$ 0 months; 85 days $\to$ 2 months; 145 days $\to$ 4 months).
    \item \textbf{Descending Swap Margins (Foreign Currency at Forward Discount):}
    Deducting a discount lowers the buying rate. To ensure the bank is adequately protected against market depreciation, the bank must \textbf{round up} the total duration to the \textbf{higher whole month}.
    \item \textbf{Exchange Margin Deduction:}
    The standard merchant loading percentage (e.g., $0.10\%$) is computed on the base buying rate and deducted.
    \item \textbf{FEDAI Rounding Rule:}
    The resulting merchant quote is rounded off to the nearest multiple of $0.0025$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Option Forward Contracts and Delivery Window Margin Rules}
\lecturedate{12/10/2026}
Under FEDAI Rule 7, an option forward contract provides the customer with operational flexibility to execute delivery on any working day within an agreed calendar window (not exceeding one month). 

Because the exact date of settlement is elected exclusively by the merchant, the quoting bank faces uncertainty regarding the exact interbank swap points to apply. To eliminate counterparty selection risk, FEDAI establishes the \textbf{Worst-Of Principle}:

\begin{center}
\begin{minipage}{0.92\linewidth}
\begin{remarkbox}[FEDAI "Worst-Of" Principle for Option Contracts]
The quoting bank must always compute the merchant forward rate on the assumption that the customer will elect to deliver foreign exchange on the specific calendar date within the option window that is \textbf{least advantageous to the bank} (and most favorable to the customer).
\end{remarkbox}
\end{minipage}
\end{center}

\subsubsection{Statutory Delivery Date Selection Protocol}
The operational date assumed by the bank depends on whether the transaction is a bank purchase or bank sale, and whether the currency is at a premium or discount:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Forward Purchase by Bank (Customer Sells Foreign Exchange):}
    \begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
        \item \textit{Foreign Currency at Premium:} Adding premium increases the payout to the customer. Therefore, the bank quotes the \textbf{Earliest Delivery Date} of the option window, giving the customer the lowest premium.
        \item \textit{Foreign Currency at Discount:} Deducting discount reduces the payout. Therefore, the bank quotes the \textbf{Latest Delivery Date} of the option window, deducting the maximum discount.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Forward Sale by Bank (Customer Buys Foreign Exchange):}
    \begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
        \item \textit{Foreign Currency at Premium:} Adding premium increases the cost to the customer. Therefore, the bank quotes the \textbf{Latest Delivery Date} of the option window, charging the maximum premium.
        \item \textit{Foreign Currency at Discount:} Deducting discount reduces the cost. Therefore, the bank quotes the \textbf{Earliest Delivery Date} of the option window, granting the minimum discount.
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Theories of Foreign Exchange Rate Determination}
\lecturedate{21/10/2026}

Understanding the macroeconomic forces that dictate exchange rate movements is essential for corporate treasury operations, cross-border pricing, and international investment strategy. Exchange rate determination models formalize the fundamental relationships linking money supply growth, price level inflation, interest rate differentials, and forward foreign exchange margins.

\subsection{The Law of One Price and Purchasing Power Parity (PPP)}
The foundational premise of foreign exchange valuation is the \textbf{Law of One Price (LOOP)}. It postulates that in frictionless international markets---absent transportation costs, import tariffs, and trade barriers---an identical basket of tradable goods must sell for the exact same price across different countries when converted into a common currency:
\begin{equation}
    P_d = \Spot \times P_f \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \Spot = \frac{P_d}{P_f}
\end{equation}
where $P_d$ represents the domestic currency price, $P_f$ is the foreign currency price, and $\Spot$ is the spot exchange rate quoted in direct terms (domestic currency units per unit of foreign currency).

\subsubsection{Classroom Commodity Basket Illustration}
Consider a standardized consumer commodity (e.g., $1\,\text{kg}$ of oranges) whose market price is observed in India and the United States:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, noitemsep]
    \item At baseline $t=0$, $1\,\text{kg}$ of oranges costs $\inr 60$ in India and $\$1.00$ in the US.
    \item Under the Law of One Price, the implied equilibrium exchange rate is:
    \begin{equation*}
        \inr 60 = 1\,\text{kg orange} = \$1.00 \implies \Spot_0 = \inr 60\,/\$
    \end{equation*}
\end{itemize}

When inflation rates diverge between the two economies, the purchasing power of money erodes unevenly. Suppose an extended consumption basket initially costs $\inr 9{,}000$ in India and $\$100$ in the United States, establishing the spot rate $\Spot_0 = \frac{9{,}000}{100} = \inr 90\,/\$$. Over an annual period, the annual rate of inflation is $8\%$ in India and $5\%$ in the United States:
\begin{align*}
    P_{d, 1} &= \inr 9{,}000 \times (1 + 0.08) = \inr 9{,}720 \\
    P_{f, 1} &= \$100 \times (1 + 0.05) = \$105
\end{align*}
To restore purchasing power equivalence, the revised exchange rate $\Spot_1$ must adjust to:
\begin{equation*}
    \Spot_1 = \frac{\inr 9{,}720}{\$105} = \mathbf{\inr 92.57\,/\$}
\end{equation*}
The Indian Rupee depreciates by $\inr 92.57 - \inr 90.00 = \inr 2.57\,/\$$, exactly offsetting the $3\%$ inflation differential ($8\% - 5\%$) between the two trading partners.

\subsubsection{Absolute vs. Relative Purchasing Power Parity}
Economists distinguish between two formal variants of PPP:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, label=\arabic*.]
    \item \textbf{Absolute Purchasing Power Parity:} Requires that the spot exchange rate directly equals the ratio of the overall national price level indexes:
    \begin{equation}
        \Spot_t = \frac{P_{d, t}}{P_{f, t}}
    \end{equation}
    Absolute PPP rarely holds strictly in empirical reality due to transport costs, tariffs, non-traded services, and differing consumer preferences across countries.
    \item \textbf{Relative Purchasing Power Parity:} Relaxes the requirement of identical absolute price levels and instead asserts that the \textit{percentage change} in the spot exchange rate over any time horizon $t$ is determined by the inflation differential between the domestic and foreign economies:
    \begin{equation}
        \Spot_t = \Spot_0 \left( \frac{1 + i_L}{1 + i_F} \right)^t
    \end{equation}
    where $i_L$ is the domestic (local) inflation rate, $i_F$ is the foreign inflation rate, and $t$ is the time horizon in years. If the domestic country experiences higher inflation than the foreign partner ($i_L > i_F$), the domestic currency must depreciate relative to the foreign currency to preserve export competitiveness.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, label=\arabic*.]
    \item \textbf{Monetary Theory of Inflation:} When the domestic supply of money expands at a rate faster than real output demand, the purchasing power of the currency declines, generating generalized price inflation ($\Delta M^s \to \Delta \pi$).
    \item \textbf{Purchasing Power Parity (PPP):} Cross-border goods arbitrage translates higher relative inflation into an expected currency depreciation ($\Delta \pi \to \Delta S$).
    \item \textbf{The Fisher Effect:} Lenders demand higher nominal interest rates to compensate for anticipated inflation, driving the domestic nominal yield above foreign yields ($\Delta \pi \leftrightarrow \Delta i$).
    \item \textbf{International Fisher Effect (IFE):} Capital flows equalize expected returns across borders, ensuring that nominal interest rate differentials reflect expected exchange rate movements ($\Delta S \leftrightarrow \Delta i$).
    \item \textbf{Forward Rate Unbiasedness:} Under market efficiency, the forward exchange rate serves as an unbiased predictor of the future spot rate ($\Delta S \to \frac{F-S}{S}$).
    \item \textbf{Covered Interest Parity (CIP):} Covered interest arbitrage eliminates riskless arbitrage between money market rate differentials and forward exchange rate swap points ($\Delta i \leftrightarrow \frac{F-S}{S}$).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The Fisher Effect and Monetary Inflation Dynamics}
The \textbf{Fisher Effect} establishes that nominal interest rates in any economy encompass two distinct components: a real rate of return and an inflation compensation premium:
\begin{equation}
    1 + r_{\text{nominal}} = (1 + r_{\text{real}})(1 + i_{\text{inflation}})
\end{equation}
Expanding this identity yields:
\begin{equation*}
    r_{\text{nominal}} = r_{\text{real}} + i_{\text{inflation}} + (r_{\text{real}} \times i_{\text{inflation}})
\end{equation*}
Because the product term $(r_{\text{real}} \times i_{\text{inflation}})$ is negligible under moderate inflation environments, the classical linear Fisher approximation is widely applied:
\begin{equation}
    r_{\text{nominal}} \approx r_{\text{real}} + i_{\text{inflation}}
\end{equation}
Under integrated global capital markets with free financial mobility, real rates of return tend toward international convergence ($r_{\text{real}, d} \approx r_{\text{real}, f}$). Consequently, the nominal interest rate differential between two countries directly reflects their expected inflation differential:
\begin{equation}
    r_d - r_f \approx \pi_d - \pi_f
\end{equation}

\begin{example}{One-Year and Two-Year Exchange Rate Forecasting via Relative PPP}{ex_ppp_inr_usd}
\textbf{Problem Statement:}\\
The expected annual rate of inflation in India is $5\%$ and in the United States is $3\%$. If the initial spot exchange rate is $\inr 97\,/\$$, calculate the expected exchange rate:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), noitemsep]
    \item After one year ($t=1$), and
    \item After two years ($t=2$).
\end{enumerate}

\tcblower
\textbf{Solution:}\\
\noindent\textbf{1. Theoretical Formulation:}\\
Under the Relative Purchasing Power Parity framework:
\begin{equation*}
    \Spot_t = \Spot_0 \left( \frac{1 + i_L}{1 + i_F} \right)^t
\end{equation*}
Given:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, noitemsep]
    \item Base Spot Rate $\Spot_0 = \inr 97.00\,/\$$
    \item Domestic (India) Inflation $i_L = 5\% = 0.05$
    \item Foreign (USA) Inflation $i_F = 3\% = 0.03$
    \item Relative Inflation Factor $= \frac{1 + 0.05}{1 + 0.03} = \frac{1.05}{1.03} \approx 1.019417$
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{2. Step-by-Step Forecast Calculation:}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Exchange Rate after One Year ($t=1$):}
    \begin{align*}
        \Spot_1 &= 97.00 \times \left( \frac{1.05}{1.03} \right)^1 = 97.00 \times 1.019417 \\
                &= \mathbf{\inr 98.88\,/\$}
    \end{align*}

    \end{align*}

\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Interest Rate Parity (IRP) and Covered Interest Arbitrage}
\lecturedate{26/10/2026}
While Purchasing Power Parity connects goods markets through commodity arbitrage over medium-to-long horizons, \textbf{Interest Rate Parity (IRP)} governs financial markets through money market arbitrage over short-to-medium horizons.

\subsubsection{Benchmark Spot and Forward Outright Quotations}
In foreign exchange operations:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Spot Rate ($\Spot$):} The outright benchmark rate for immediate delivery ($T+2$ business days).
    \item \textbf{Forward Rate ($\Forward$):} The contracted rate established today for delivery and settlement at a designated future maturity date ($T > 2$).
\end{itemize}
Under market equilibrium, the percentage forward premium or discount on a foreign currency equals the nominal interest rate differential between the domestic and foreign financial centers:
\begin{equation}
    \Forward = \Spot \times \left( \frac{1 + i_d}{1 + i_f} \right)
\end{equation}
where $i_d$ is the domestic interest rate and $i_f$ is the foreign interest rate for the matching tenor. Subtracting $\Spot$ from both sides yields the classical forward premium relationship:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\Forward - \Spot}{\Spot} = \frac{i_d - i_f}{1 + i_f} \approx i_d - i_f
\end{equation}
The currency associated with the higher nominal interest rate trades at a forward discount, while the currency with the lower nominal interest rate trades at a forward premium.

\subsubsection{Covered Interest Arbitrage Mechanics}
When market forward rates deviate from the theoretical parity condition defined by \Cref{fig:macroeconomic_parity_architecture}, an arbitrageur can execute a four-stage riskless transaction sequence without bearing foreign exchange risk:

\clearpage
\begin{example}{Covered Interest Arbitrage Execution and CIP Verification}{ex_covered_interest_arbitrage}
\textbf{Problem Statement:}\\
An arbitrageur observes 1-year US interest rate $i_{\text{US}} = 5.0\%$, UK interest rate $i_{\text{UK}} = 8.0\%$, spot rate $\Spot = \$1.50\,/\text{GBP}$, and 1-year forward rate $\Forward = \$1.48\,/\text{GBP}$. Demonstrate how borrowing $\$1{,}000{,}000$ in the US to execute covered interest arbitrage yields a net riskless arbitrage profit, and contrast the market forward rate against the theoretical Covered Interest Parity (CIP) benchmark.

\tcblower
\textbf{Solution:}\\
\noindent\textbf{1. Four-Stage Covered Interest Arbitrage Sequence:}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=1.5pt, topsep=1.5pt]
    \item \textbf{Stage 1 (Domestic Borrowing \& Spot Conversion, $t=0$):} Borrow $\$1{,}000{,}000$ in the US at $5\%$ and convert to Sterling at $\Spot = \$1.50\,/\text{GBP} \implies \text{Sterling Proceeds} = \frac{\$1{,}000{,}000}{1.50} = \mathbf{\gbp 666{,}666.67}$.
    \item \textbf{Stage 2 (Foreign Money Market Investment, $t=0$ to $t=1$):} Invest $\gbp 666{,}666.67$ in the UK at $8\% \implies \text{Maturity Value} = \gbp 666{,}666.67 \times 1.08 = \mathbf{\gbp 720{,}000.00}$.
    \item \textbf{Stage 3 (Forward Exchange Cover, $t=0$ locked):} Simultaneously sell $\gbp 720{,}000$ forward at $\Forward = \$1.48\,/\text{GBP} \implies \text{Forward Inflow} = \gbp 720{,}000 \times 1.48 = \mathbf{\$1{,}065{,}600.00}$.
    \item \textbf{Stage 4 (Loan Repayment \& Profit Extraction, $t=1$):} Repay US debt: $\$1{,}000{,}000 \times 1.05 = \mathbf{\$1{,}050{,}000.00}$. Net riskless profit: $\$1{,}065{,}600 - \$1{,}050{,}000 = \mathbf{\$15{,}600.00}$.
\end{itemize}
